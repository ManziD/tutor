The topics that trip up most O-Level students, and how targeted coaching closes the gaps.

Frequently Asked Questions

Passing O-Level Physics comes down to three things: understanding concepts rather than memorizing them, practicing past papers under timed conditions, and getting targeted help on the topics that trip you up most — usually mechanics, electricity, and waves. Consistent practice over months, not last-minute cramming, is what separates a pass from a strong grade.

A-Level Physics is harder than O-Level because it demands stronger mathematical reasoning, deeper conceptual understanding, and the ability to apply multiple ideas to a single problem rather than recall a single fact. Most students struggle not because the content is impossible, but because they never fully closed the gaps from O-Level before moving on.

The best preparation combines a solid grasp of the fundamentals (algebra, calculus, trigonometry) with regular past-paper practice under exam conditions. Working through UNEB-style questions with feedback on your specific mistakes is far more effective than passively reading notes or textbooks.

Around 5–10 hours a week, spread across several focused sessions rather than one long cram, works best — consistency matters far more than total hours.

Yes — a weak starting point in Mathematics is rarely about ability; it's usually about gaps in earlier topics that were never fully closed. With focused coaching that identifies and fixes those specific gaps, most students can significantly improve their grade well before their final exams.

Students most often struggle with mechanics (forces, motion, and momentum), electricity, and any topic involving multi-step calculations. These topics build on each other, so falling behind early tends to compound — which is why early, targeted support makes the biggest difference.

Pure Mathematics focuses on abstract mathematical concepts like algebra, calculus, and geometry, while Applied Mathematics uses those tools to solve real-world problems in areas like mechanics and statistics. Read the full breakdown →

Subsidiary Mathematics can be taken alongside three principal subjects other than Principal Mathematics, while Principal Mathematics is itself a principal subject at A-Level, preparing students for courses that require strong mathematical grounding such as Engineering, Medicine, and Information Technology.

Focus on Algebra, Calculus, and Trigonometry in Pure Mathematics, along with Statistics and Mechanics in Applied Mathematics.

Improving at Physics calculations comes from practicing a wide variety of problems, not just repeating the same question types. Focus on understanding why a formula applies to a situation, not just plugging in numbers — that's what allows you to handle unfamiliar questions in the actual exam.

Work through past papers under real exam time limits, then go back and understand why you got each wrong answer wrong — not just what the correct answer was. Repeating this process across multiple years of past papers builds both speed and exam-specific pattern recognition.

Most "careless" mistakes are actually a rushing problem — solve the problem, then check units, signs, and whether your answer makes sense before moving on. Slowing down slightly on each question usually saves more time than it costs, since it prevents costly re-work.

Understanding where a formula comes from makes it far easier to remember and apply correctly — memorized formulas without understanding tend to fall apart on unfamiliar exam questions. Aim to understand the derivation first; the memorization follows naturally.
